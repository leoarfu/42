The Radio Access Network (RAN): From Absolute Zero

Every mobile phone call, text message, video stream, or satellite NTN session begins inside the Radio Access Network. The RAN is the part of the mobile network responsible for transporting information between the user's device and the operator's core network using radio waves.

A common interview mistake is saying "the RAN is the antenna." It is not. The antenna is only one physical component. The RAN is an entire distributed computing system made of radios, digital processors, scheduling software, synchronization, and transport networks.

FIRST PRINCIPLE

The RAN converts electromagnetic waves into digital information, makes real-time scheduling decisions every millisecond, manages mobility, and delivers packets toward the 5G Core.

The complete 5G architecture

Everything left of the 5G Core is considered the RAN. The Core provides network services; the RAN provides radio access.

What exactly is the UE?

UE
User Equipment. Any device connecting to the mobile network: smartphone, IoT sensor, aircraft terminal, vehicle modem, or NTN-capable handset.
Examples
iPhone, Samsung Galaxy, Starlink NTN phone, industrial modem, connected vehicle gateway.
Contains
RF transceiver, baseband modem, antennas, SIM/eSIM, GNSS receiver, application processor.

Interviewers often use UE instead of "phone." Use the acronym naturally.

What is a gNB?

The gNB (Next Generation Node B) is the 5G base station. In LTE, the equivalent was called an eNodeB; in 5G it becomes the gNB.

4G 5G
eNodeB gNB
LTE NR
EPC 5GC

The important insight is that the gNB is not a single box. Modern 5G splits it into several functional blocks.

Why split the gNB?

Earlier cellular generations placed almost everything inside one cabinet at the cell tower. 5G separates time-critical processing from higher-layer functions so operators can virtualize, centralize, and scale the network independently.

INTERVIEW ANSWER

A gNB is the logical 5G base station. In practice it is usually implemented as a distributed architecture composed of an RU, a DU, and a CU connected through standardized interfaces.

The Protocol Stack: The Most Important Diagram in 5G

A protocol stack is a hierarchy of software layers. Each layer performs one specific job and communicates only with the layers immediately above and below it.

Think of it like a postal service: one layer writes the letter, another encrypts it, another places it in an envelope, another chooses the delivery route, and another physically transports it.

The complete user-plane stack

Layer-by-layer explanation

PHY
Physical Layer. Converts digital bits into OFDM radio symbols, performs modulation, coding, synchronization, beamforming, and RF transmission.
MAC
Medium Access Control. Decides which UE transmits, when it transmits, and on which resource blocks. This is the scheduler.
RLC
Radio Link Control. Segmentation, reassembly, buffering, retransmission, and reliable radio delivery.
PDCP
Packet Data Convergence Protocol. Ciphering, integrity protection, packet numbering, and duplication for robustness.
SDAP
Service Data Adaptation Protocol. Maps application traffic onto QoS flows.

Where do these layers live?

Notice something important: the protocol stack is physically distributed. The lower layers stay close to the radio because they have strict real-time deadlines, while higher layers can be centralized inside data centers.

  • PHY generates OFDM symbols and controls the radio.
  • MAC schedules every transmission opportunity.
  • RLC provides reliable radio delivery.
  • PDCP encrypts and protects packets.
  • SDAP maps QoS flows to radio bearers.

Interview question

The MAC scheduler makes transmission decisions every slot (often every millisecond or less). Those deadlines are too strict to tolerate long transport latency. Therefore MAC is placed inside the Distributed Unit, close to the radio, while higher-layer protocols can be centralized in the CU.

O-RAN — The Open Radio Access Network

Traditional mobile networks were largely proprietary: one vendor supplied the radio, baseband, management software and interfaces. O-RAN (Open Radio Access Network) standardizes those interfaces so equipment from different vendors can interoperate.

INTERVIEW ANSWER

O-RAN is not a new radio technology. The air interface is still 3GPP NR. O-RAN defines open, interoperable architecture and management interfaces around the RAN.

The complete O-RAN picture

Why was O-RAN created?

Imagine buying a laptop where the keyboard, screen and battery could only come from one manufacturer. That was effectively the historical RAN market.

O-RAN separates hardware from software through standardized interfaces. Operators can combine radios, baseband software and orchestration platforms from different vendors.

Traditional RAN O-RAN
Single vendor ecosystem Multi-vendor interoperability
Proprietary interfaces Open standardized interfaces
Tightly coupled hardware Virtualized software functions
Limited innovation xApps and rApps extend functionality

What does the "O" mean?

O-RU
Open Radio Unit. RF hardware, antennas, power amplifiers and low-PHY processing.
O-DU
Open Distributed Unit. Real-time MAC scheduler, HARQ, RLC and high-PHY functions.
O-CU
Open Central Unit. PDCP, SDAP, RRC and higher-layer control.

The Radio Unit (RU)

The RU is the only component directly connected to the antenna. It converts between digital baseband samples and analog RF signals.

A useful interview phrase is:

SOUND TECHNICAL

"The RU is primarily responsible for RF transmission and reception, digital-to-analog conversion, analog-to-digital conversion, beamforming, and low-PHY functions."

The Distributed Unit (DU)

The DU is where the network makes its fastest decisions. Every slot, it determines which UE receives resources, performs HARQ processing and manages radio buffers.

DU Responsibilities

  • MAC scheduling
  • HARQ processing
  • RLC segmentation
  • Buffer management
  • Real-time radio control
  • High-PHY processing

Because these operations occur every millisecond or less, the DU is usually deployed physically close to the RU.

The Central Unit (CU)

The CU manages higher-layer protocols and mobility. Unlike the DU, it is not constrained by extremely low latency and can therefore be centralized inside regional data centers.

CU-CP vs CU-UP

CU-CP CU-UP
Control Plane User Plane
RRC PDCP
Mobility User traffic
Bearer setup Packet forwarding
Signaling QoS delivery

This separation allows operators to scale signaling and user traffic independently.

The interfaces everyone asks about

F1
Interface between DU and CU.
E1
Interface between CU-CP and CU-UP.
NG
Interface between CU and the 5G Core.
Open Fronthaul
Interface between O-RU and O-DU.
E2
Near-RT RIC interface toward the DU.
A1
Policy interface between Non-RT RIC and Near-RT RIC.
O1
Management interface used by the SMO.

Fronthaul vs Midhaul vs Backhaul

A common interview question is simply: "Explain the difference."

  • Fronthaul: RU ↔ DU (highest bandwidth, lowest latency)
  • Midhaul: DU ↔ CU
  • Backhaul: CU ↔ 5G Core

The RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC)

The RIC does not transmit radio signals. It observes the RAN, analyzes telemetry and optimizes behavior through software applications.

Near-RT vs Non-RT

Near-RT RIC
Operates roughly between 10 milliseconds and 1 second. Performs congestion optimization, interference mitigation and mobility tuning through xApps.
Non-RT RIC
Operates above 1 second. Builds policies, long-term optimization and machine-learning models through rApps.
xApp
Application running inside the Near-RT RIC.
rApp
Application running inside the Non-RT RIC.

Service Management & Orchestration (SMO)

The SMO sits above the RAN. It deploys software, monitors alarms, provisions configuration, collects performance metrics and manages lifecycle operations across thousands of radios.

ELEVATOR PITCH

If the DU is the real-time scheduler and the CU is the higher-layer controller, the SMO is the network's operations platform. It is where operators configure, monitor and orchestrate the entire RAN.

  • RU = RF and antennas
  • DU = Real-time scheduling
  • CU = Higher-layer control
  • F1 = DU ↔ CU
  • E1 = CU-CP ↔ CU-UP
  • NG = CU ↔ 5G Core
  • RIC = Intelligent optimization
  • SMO = Lifecycle & orchestration

Because different functions have different latency requirements. RF and real-time scheduling remain close to the antenna in the RU and DU, while higher-layer protocols can be centralized in the CU. This enables virtualization, cloud deployment, independent scaling and multi-vendor interoperability through O-RAN.

5G NTN — Non-Terrestrial Networks

The most important interview realization is this: NTN is not "5G over satellite." It is 3GPP's redesign of the Radio Access Network so that a base station can exist hundreds of kilometers above Earth while remaining compatible with the 5G ecosystem.

ONE SENTENCE

NTN extends the 5G RAN to operate through satellites by compensating for long propagation delay, high Doppler shift, moving cells, and rapidly changing geometry.

What does NTN actually mean?

TermMeaning
TNTerrestrial Network
NTNNon-Terrestrial Network
NR-NTN5G New Radio adapted for satellites
Rel-17First major 3GPP NTN release

The word non-terrestrial includes LEO, MEO, GEO satellites and even High Altitude Platform Stations (HAPS). The interview role is primarily concerned with LEO NTN.

---

Why terrestrial 5G breaks in space

A terrestrial base station assumes four things that immediately become false in LEO.

Terrestrial assumption LEO reality
Static base station Satellite moves at ~7.6 km/s
Short propagation delay Hundreds of kilometers
Small Doppler Tens of kHz
Cell stays in place Beam continuously moves
---

The complete NTN architecture

Notice there are two completely different radio links.

Service Link
The radio connection between the UE and the satellite.
Feeder Link
The high-capacity connection between the satellite and the gateway.

Interviewers often ask which link is LTE/NR. The answer is: the service link. The feeder link may use entirely different frequencies and technologies.

---

Transparent vs Regenerative Payload

Transparent (Bent-Pipe)

The satellite performs almost no baseband processing. It simply receives RF energy, shifts frequency, amplifies it, and forwards it to the gateway. The actual gNB remains on Earth.

Regenerative Payload

Transparent Regenerative
Satellite is RF relay Satellite processes baseband
Ground gNB Onboard gNB possible
Simpler payload More powerful payload
Gateway dependent Greater autonomy
INTERVIEW PHRASE

Transparent payloads move intelligence to the ground. Regenerative payloads move part of the RAN into space.

---

Where is the gNB located?

This is one of the most common architectural questions.

Notice the distinction between the logical gNB and the physical satellite. The interview is really testing where the RAN functions execute.

---

Timing Advance

Radio transmissions from many UEs must arrive at the base station at precisely aligned instants. Otherwise signals overlap and interfere with one another.

In terrestrial networks, the base station measures arrival time and tells the UE how much earlier or later to transmit.

In NTN, propagation delay is much larger, and the geometry changes continuously. Rel-17 therefore allows the UE to calculate part of the timing correction using GNSS and satellite trajectory information.

---

Doppler Compensation

As the satellite approaches, the received frequency appears higher. As it moves away, the frequency appears lower.

The Doppler shift is approximately

vᵣ
Radial velocity between satellite and UE.
fᶜ
Carrier frequency.
c
Speed of light.

At approximately 1.9 GHz, a LEO satellite can produce roughly ±48 kHz of Doppler shift. The exact value depends on radial velocity, not orbital speed alone.

WHY IT MATTERS

OFDM depends on subcarrier orthogonality. Doppler shifts the received spectrum and creates inter-carrier interference if not compensated.

---

K_offset — Honest Scheduling

A terrestrial scheduler assumes control messages arrive almost immediately. That assumption is false over satellite.

Rather than pretending the delay does not exist, NTN explicitly tells the UE how far into the future a scheduled transmission should occur. That scheduling offset is represented by K_offset.

---

HARQ in NTN

HARQ (Hybrid Automatic Repeat Request) is a retransmission mechanism. In terrestrial networks it works extremely well because acknowledgements return quickly.

Terrestrial NTN
Fast ACK Long RTT
Few packets in flight Many simultaneous packets
Immediate retransmission Relaxed or disabled HARQ

The important engineering idea is not that HARQ disappears. It is that the protocol must tolerate much longer acknowledgement cycles.

---

GNSS & Ephemeris

GNSS
Global Navigation Satellite System. GPS, Galileo, BeiDou and GLONASS are all GNSS constellations.
Ephemeris
Orbital position and trajectory information broadcast by the satellite so the UE knows where the satellite is.

A Rel-17 NTN handset combines its own GNSS position with satellite ephemeris to estimate propagation delay and Doppler before transmission.

---

What changes in Rel-17?

Feature Purpose
GNSS support UE knows its position
Ephemeris Satellite trajectory broadcast
Timing compensation Large propagation delay
Doppler compensation Moving satellite
K_offset Future scheduling
Relaxed HARQ Long RTT operation
  • Service link = UE ↔ Satellite
  • Feeder link = Satellite ↔ Gateway
  • Transparent = RF relay only
  • Regenerative = Baseband processing onboard
  • Timing Advance = Align uplink arrivals
  • Doppler Compensation = Preserve OFDM orthogonality
  • K_offset = Future-aware scheduling
  • Ephemeris = Satellite orbital data

A transparent payload behaves like an RF repeater: it forwards the waveform to a gateway where the gNB remains on Earth. A regenerative payload performs part of the RAN processing onboard the satellite, potentially including MAC, RLC or even the gNB itself. The tradeoff is simpler payload and gateway dependence versus greater onboard capability and autonomy.

Cloud RAN, NFV & Virtualization

Modern 5G networks are no longer built from proprietary hardware appliances. The CU, DU, RIC and many core functions increasingly run as software on standardized servers inside data centres or edge computing sites. This transformation is called virtualization.

INTERVIEW ANSWER

Virtualization separates network functions from the underlying hardware. Instead of buying a dedicated baseband appliance, operators deploy software functions on commercial compute infrastructure.

---

From Hardware to Software

The radio hardware still exists. What changes is that much of the baseband intelligence becomes software.

---

What is NFV?

NFV
Network Function Virtualization. The concept of implementing network functions as software rather than dedicated hardware.
Goal
Reduce hardware dependence, improve scalability, accelerate deployment and simplify lifecycle management.

NFV is a design philosophy. It is not a specific product.

Example

Before NFV After NFV
Physical EPC appliance Virtual AMF
Dedicated baseband cabinet Virtual DU
Hardware mobility server Containerized CU
---

VNF vs CNF

These two acronyms appear constantly in telecom interviews.

VNF CNF
Virtual Network Function Cloud Native Network Function
Runs inside VMs Runs in containers
Heavier Lighter
Hypervisor Kubernetes
MEMORIZE

A VNF is virtualized. A CNF is containerized. Cloud-native 5G increasingly favors CNFs.

---

What is a Container?

A container packages an application together with its libraries while sharing the host operating system. Unlike a virtual machine, it does not require its own complete operating system.

Think of containers as lightweight isolated applications sharing the same Linux kernel.

---

Kubernetes — The Telecom Orchestrator

Running one container is easy. Running ten thousand across hundreds of edge sites is not. Kubernetes solves that orchestration problem.

Kubernetes Responsibilities

  • Deploy containers automatically
  • Restart failed applications
  • Scale CU and DU instances
  • Perform rolling software updates
  • Distribute workloads across servers
  • Monitor health of network functions
---

Helm — The Package Manager

If Kubernetes is the operating system of cloud infrastructure, Helm is its application installer.

Helm
A package manager for Kubernetes that deploys complete applications using reusable templates called charts.
---

What is O-Cloud?

O-Cloud is the cloud infrastructure standardized by O-RAN on which CU, DU, RIC and management software execute.

Interview phrasing:

GOOD ANSWER

O-Cloud is the standardized cloud infrastructure layer defined by O-RAN that provides compute, storage, networking and virtualization resources for cloud-native RAN functions.

---

Edge Computing vs Central Cloud

Why not place every DU in one massive data centre? Because latency matters.

Edge Regional Cloud
DU CU
Low latency Large compute capacity
Near antenna Centralized operations

Why Capgemini Cares

The integration role explicitly mentions virtualization, NFV, containers and orchestration because the engineer is expected to integrate software RAN functions across distributed cloud infrastructure—not simply install radios.

  • NFV = Network Function Virtualization
  • VNF = Virtual Machine-based function
  • CNF = Containerized function
  • Kubernetes = Container orchestrator
  • Helm = Kubernetes package manager
  • O-Cloud = Cloud infrastructure for O-RAN
  • Cloud RAN = Software-based RAN deployment

The DU contains the MAC scheduler and real-time PHY processing, which have strict latency deadlines. Placing it near the RU minimizes transport latency. The CU handles higher-layer protocols such as PDCP and RRC, which tolerate more latency and benefit from centralized cloud deployment.

Beam Management & Mobility

A terrestrial cell is usually identified by a fixed tower. A LEO NTN cell is fundamentally different: the satellite moves continuously, its beams move across Earth, and the UE must remain synchronized while the serving beam changes.

KEY IDEA

In 5G, mobility is no longer only about moving users. In NTN, the network itself is moving.

What is a Beam?

A beam is a focused region of radio energy created by an antenna array. Instead of illuminating the entire Earth equally, modern satellites generate dozens or even hundreds of narrow steerable beams.

Each beam behaves similarly to a cellular sector: it has its own synchronization, scheduling resources, and capacity.

---

Why Multiple Beams?

Single Wide Beam Many Narrow Beams
Low capacity High frequency reuse
Weak signal Higher antenna gain
Poor spatial reuse Independent scheduling
Large interference area Smaller interference footprint
INTERVIEW INSIGHT

Narrow beams increase capacity because the same spectrum can be reused in geographically separated beams.

Beamforming

Beamforming is the process of electronically steering radio energy without mechanically rotating the antenna.

Instead of transmitting equally in every direction, the antenna adjusts the phase of each antenna element so the waves add constructively in one direction.

Beamforming
Electronic steering of radio energy by controlling the phase and amplitude of multiple antenna elements.
Phased Array
An antenna composed of many small radiating elements whose phases can be independently controlled.
---

Beam Sweeping

Before a UE can connect, it must discover which beam is strongest. The satellite therefore transmits synchronization signals across many beams sequentially.

The UE measures each synchronization transmission and reports the best candidate to the network.

---

SSB — Synchronization Signal Block

The Synchronization Signal Block (SSB) is the first thing a UE looks for when searching for a cell.

SSB
A predefined OFDM structure carrying synchronization and basic broadcast information so a UE can detect and synchronize with a cell.
Contains
PSS, SSS and PBCH.

The three parts of an SSB

Signal Purpose
PSS Primary synchronization
SSS Secondary synchronization
PBCH Broadcast system information
MEMORIZE

PSS finds timing, SSS identifies the cell, and PBCH tells the UE how to access the network.

---

CSI-RS — Measuring the Radio Channel

Once connected, synchronization is no longer enough. The network needs continuous measurements of channel quality.

CSI
Channel State Information.
CSI-RS
Reference signals transmitted so the UE can estimate channel quality, beam quality and MIMO performance.
SSB CSI-RS
Initial access Connected mode
Find the cell Measure channel quality
Synchronization Beam optimization
---

Beam Tracking

A LEO satellite moves continuously. The UE therefore tracks the best beam throughout the pass.

The serving beam changes even if the UE is standing perfectly still.

---

Beam Handover vs Satellite Handover

These are frequently confused in interviews.

Beam Handover

The satellite remains the same. Only the serving beam changes.

Satellite Handover

Here, the serving spacecraft itself changes. This is generally a more complex mobility procedure.

Beam Handover Satellite Handover
Same satellite Different satellite
Different beam Different spacecraft
Frequent Less frequent
Lower signaling overhead Higher coordination complexity
---

Beam Hopping

Some satellite systems do not illuminate every beam continuously. Instead, power is dynamically allocated where traffic exists.

Beam Hopping
A resource allocation technique where satellite power is rapidly switched between beams instead of transmitting continuously everywhere.
---

The Complete Mobility Timeline

  1. UE searches for synchronization signals.
  2. SSB identifies the best beam.
  3. Random access establishes the connection.
  4. CSI-RS measures channel quality.
  5. Beam tracking maintains the strongest beam.
  6. Beam or satellite handover preserves service continuity.
---

Mobility KPIs

KPI Meaning
RSRP Reference Signal Received Power
RSRQ Reference Signal Received Quality
SINR Signal-to-Interference-plus-Noise Ratio
Handover Success Rate Successful mobility procedures
Beam Failure Rate Loss of serving beam
Access Delay Time to establish connection
GOOD INTERVIEW ANSWER

"I would monitor RSRP, RSRQ and SINR for radio quality, beam failure events for mobility robustness, and handover success rate to evaluate continuity across moving NTN beams."

---

Know Cold

  • Beam = Focused region of RF coverage
  • Beamforming = Electronic steering of radio energy
  • Phased Array = Multi-element steerable antenna
  • SSB = Synchronization Signal Block
  • PSS = Primary Synchronization Signal
  • SSS = Secondary Synchronization Signal
  • PBCH = Physical Broadcast Channel
  • CSI-RS = Channel State Information Reference Signal
  • Beam Tracking = Continuous optimization of serving beam
  • Beam Hopping = Dynamic allocation of satellite power
  • Beam Handover = Same satellite, different beam
  • Satellite Handover = Different spacecraft

SSB is used during initial access. It allows the UE to detect a cell, synchronize in time and frequency, and receive broadcast information through PSS, SSS and PBCH. CSI-RS is used after connection establishment so the UE can measure channel quality, evaluate beam quality and support beam management and MIMO optimization.

A stationary UE should still experience beam mobility because the satellite is moving. I would first investigate beam tracking and beam handover performance by examining SSB measurements, CSI-RS reports, beam failure events and handover success logs before suspecting RF hardware.

Integration Engineering

The Capgemini role is not primarily about designing algorithms. It is about making equipment from multiple vendors operate together inside a complete 5G NTN system. Integration engineers live at the boundary between RF, software, networking, cloud infrastructure and system validation.

INTERVIEW ANSWER

Integration is the discipline of verifying that independently developed subsystems operate correctly together under realistic operational conditions.

---

The End-to-End System

Every interface is a potential failure point. Good integration engineers isolate which subsystem fails before proposing a fix.

---

Integration Lifecycle

Notice that testing is not the final step. Validation happens before deployment, but monitoring continues throughout operations.

The Troubleshooting Pyramid

Never begin with assumptions. Start from the highest observable symptom and progressively isolate the failing layer.

A disciplined engineer identifies the first layer where expected behavior diverges. Everything above that layer is usually a consequence rather than the root cause.

---

Root Cause Methodology

INTERVIEW ANSWER

I always reproduce the issue first, capture the exact software versions and topology, localize the failing subsystem using logs and KPIs, validate the hypothesis experimentally, and finally execute a regression campaign to ensure the correction does not introduce new failures.

---

Example Scenario 1

Symptom

A UE suddenly loses synchronization during a LEO satellite pass.

Investigation Order

  1. Check SSB detection history.
  2. Review CSI-RS measurements.
  3. Look for beam failure events.
  4. Verify Doppler compensation logs.
  5. Confirm timing advance evolution.
  6. Determine whether beam handover succeeded.

Possible Root Causes

  • Incorrect Doppler estimation
  • Beam tracking failure
  • Late timing correction
  • SSB measurement degradation
  • Satellite ephemeris inconsistency
---

Example Scenario 2

Symptom

Throughput suddenly drops while RSRP remains excellent.

Interpretation

Strong signal does not necessarily mean good channel quality.

Metric Possible Observation
RSRP Excellent
RSRQ Poor
SINR Low
BLER High

Likely causes include interference, scheduler congestion, beam overlap or poor SINR rather than insufficient received power.

---

Example Scenario 3

Symptom

A Vendor A RU refuses to operate with a Vendor B DU.

Engineering Thought Process

  1. Verify Open Fronthaul version compatibility.
  2. Check timing synchronization.
  3. Validate O-RAN profiles.
  4. Compare software releases.
  5. Capture interface traces.
  6. Reproduce using reference configuration.

This is an interoperability problem, not necessarily an RF problem.

---

What Logs Matter?

Component Useful Logs
UE RRC events, measurements
RU RF alarms, synchronization
DU MAC scheduler, HARQ
CU RRC, mobility, bearer setup
Gateway Transport latency
5GC Registration, sessions
---

Interview Case Study

I begin by determining the highest successful procedure. Did the UE detect an SSB? Did random access complete? Did RRC setup succeed? Did registration reach the AMF? This immediately localizes whether the failure is PHY, MAC, RRC or Core.

Run a regression campaign using the original passing scenarios plus beam mobility, Doppler, timing compensation and handover tests. Compare KPIs before and after the update to isolate behavioral regressions.

Telecom systems are highly interconnected. A change in scheduling, mobility or timing may unintentionally affect unrelated procedures. Regression testing verifies that previously validated behavior still conforms after modifications.

---

UE → NR air interface → RU → DU → CU → Gateway → 5G Core → UPF → Internet. In a regenerative architecture, part of the RAN may execute onboard the satellite before forwarding traffic toward the gateway.

The DU contains MAC scheduling and real-time PHY functions with millisecond deadlines. The CU contains higher-layer protocols that tolerate greater transport latency and therefore benefit from cloud centralization.

Doppler depends on radial velocity. A LEO satellite moves rapidly relative to the UE, producing large and continuously changing frequency offsets that must be compensated to preserve OFDM orthogonality.

Conformance verifies compliance with 3GPP specifications. Interoperability verifies that equipment from different vendors actually functions together across standardized interfaces.

I would examine RSRQ, SINR, BLER, scheduler utilization, HARQ statistics and interference indicators before concluding that RF coverage is the problem.

Transparent payloads relay RF to a ground gateway where the gNB resides. Regenerative payloads demodulate and process baseband onboard, moving part of the RAN into space.

The RAN Intelligent Controller optimizes network behavior through xApps and rApps. It analyzes telemetry and influences scheduling, mobility and radio optimization without directly transmitting RF signals.

It compensates for long NTN propagation delay by scheduling transmissions sufficiently far into the future so the UE receives control information before the scheduled uplink opportunity.

Review SSB measurements, CSI-RS reports, beam tracking events, timing advance evolution, Doppler estimates and handover logs to identify whether synchronization or mobility is failing first.

O-RAN enables multi-vendor interoperability through standardized interfaces, allowing operators to combine radios, distributed units, central units and management platforms instead of relying on a single proprietary ecosystem.
