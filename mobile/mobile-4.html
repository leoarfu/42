MOBILE-04

The 3GPP NTN Standard, Plainly

The full derivation — HARQ duty-factor arithmetic, the OFDM resource grid, K_offset scheduling, and a working throughput engine — lives in M5-5. This page explains what changes and why, in plain terms, before that page's notation.

1. Why 5G needed a satellite patch at all

Terrestrial NR was designed assuming cell radii of kilometres, round-trip times under a millisecond, and a UE that barely moves relative to its cell. A LEO satellite link breaks all three assumptions at once: the "cell" is a beam a thousand kilometres away, the round trip is measured in milliseconds to tens of milliseconds, and the satellite itself is moving at 7.6 km/s. None of the terrestrial standard's timing or mobility logic survives that unmodified — which is why NTN exists as a distinct 3GPP work item rather than something handled at the RF layer alone.

2. The four fixes, explained not derived

2.1 Timing — the network and the phone split the correction

A terrestrial base station tells a nearby phone exactly how much to adjust its transmit timing. At satellite range, that correction is far too large and changes too fast for the network alone to track cheaply — so NTN splits it: the network broadcasts a common correction for the whole beam, and the phone computes its own share using its own GPS position and the satellite's broadcast trajectory.

This split-compensation approach only applies to a Rel-17 NTN-capable handset — one built with the new protocol machinery. Most phones in service today, including everything Starlink Mobile currently serves, are legacy handsets: they have GPS, but none of this NTN logic. For them, the network has to do all the compensating instead, and it can only do that if each spot beam is kept small enough that the leftover timing/Doppler error across the beam stays inside what an ordinary phone's existing tracking loop can already handle without any satellite-aware software at all. This is why beam size in MOBILE-3 isn't just an antenna decision — it's also a protocol decision, forced by the fact that most phones can't help with the correction themselves.

2.2 Doppler — same split-compensation idea, plain version

The same logic applies to frequency. Part of the ~48 kHz shift from MOBILE-3 is corrected by the satellite or gateway before the signal ever reaches the phone; the rest, a Rel-17 phone corrects itself using its own position and the broadcast trajectory data — a legacy phone gets none of this and relies entirely on small beams keeping the residual error tolerable.

2.3 HARQ — why waiting for an acknowledgment doesn't work at LEO RTT

Terrestrial NR assumes a lost packet gets retransmitted and acknowledged within about a millisecond. At LEO range, that round trip is long enough that waiting for an acknowledgment before sending the next packet would waste a meaningful fraction of the link's capacity doing nothing. NTN's answer is to allow many more packets to be "in flight" unacknowledged at once — M5-5 works this out precisely with its duty-factor table — and, in some configurations, to simply turn acknowledgment off for certain traffic rather than pay that cost at all.

2.4 Scheduling — K_offset, explained as "telling the truth about how long the message takes to arrive"

A terrestrial scheduler assumes a command it sends reaches the phone almost instantly. Over a satellite link that isn't true, so NTN adds an explicit offset — how long this particular instruction will actually take to arrive — into the scheduling messages themselves, rather than pretending the terrestrial-scale assumption still holds.

3. Two sentences on the protocol stack

Everything above lives at the physical and MAC layers, plus the radio resource control layer that broadcasts the satellite's position and timing data. The layers above that — the ones responsible for reliable delivery and ciphering — mostly don't need to change at all; they just inherit a longer round trip as a number, not a redesign.

4. Transparent vs. regenerative, as the standard defines it

The standard's own vocabulary for where satellite processing happens — transparent versus regenerative — is the same distinction M3-3 covers in full, with the same tradeoffs (M3-3's noise-coupling, switching, and gateway-dependency arguments apply unchanged to the NTN case). This page only needs you to recognize the terms line up; MOBILE-5 covers what's specifically new for the D2D case.

5. Where Starlink Mobile sits relative to all of this

Explicitly: Starlink Mobile's current service runs unmodified LTE, not NR-NTN. None of the four fixes above are in play, because the phone was never told it's talking to a satellite — it's simply relayed. That's precisely why it could ship years before any standards-based NTN deployment: zero handset change, zero new standard required.

6. The 2027 convergence point

Reported plans put Starlink's Gen2 D2D satellites on the actual NR-NTN standard around 2027. If that holds, the radio-standard gap between Starlink Mobile and IRIS² narrows — though, as MOBILE-8 covers, payload architecture and core-integration philosophy could easily stay different even after both speak the same radio standard.

  • Four NTN fixes — split timing correction, split Doppler correction, relaxed/disabled HARQ, K_offset scheduling
  • Rel-17 NTN handset — computes its own timing/Doppler correction
  • Legacy handset — network does all compensating; requires small beams
  • RLC/PDCP — mostly unchanged, inherit longer RTT as a parameter
  • Transparent vs. regenerative, standard vocabulary — same as M3-3's distinction
  • Starlink Mobile today — unmodified LTE, not NR-NTN
