03 · End-to-end communication system architecture

Before discussing a waveform, understand the system in which it operates. A satellite communications system transports information from a source to a destination through a sequence of radio, digital-processing, routing and network functions.

Three main communication links

LinkConnectionEngineering concern
User linkUser terminal ↔ satelliteTerminal capability, coverage, link budget, Doppler, beam management and service quality.
Feeder linkGateway ↔ satelliteHigh-capacity transport, gateway availability, atmospheric attenuation, tracking and redundancy.
ISLSatellite ↔ satelliteInter-satellite routing, link acquisition, pointing, tracking, network topology and resilience.

Transparent versus regenerative payload

This is a fundamental architecture distinction. It determines where signal processing takes place and where the network functions reside.

TRANSPARENT / BENT-PIPE PAYLOAD

The satellite primarily receives, filters, frequency-converts, amplifies and retransmits the signal. Most baseband processing and network functions remain on the ground.

Advantages: simpler satellite processing architecture and greater reliance on established ground infrastructure.

Trade-offs: dependence on the feeder link and gateway connectivity; less onboard routing and processing flexibility.

REGENERATIVE PAYLOAD

The satellite performs some level of digital processing, potentially including demodulation, decoding, switching, routing or other network functions.

Advantages: more flexible onboard processing, potential reduction in dependence on a continuously available gateway path, and support for more advanced network architectures.

Trade-offs: increased onboard complexity, power consumption, thermal constraints, software assurance, radiation tolerance and upgrade considerations.

Where does the 5G base station reside?

Do not assume that every 5G NTN architecture places the entire gNB onboard. A gNB can be split into Central Unit (CU), Distributed Unit (DU) and Radio Unit (RU) functions, and the split may be distributed across the satellite and ground depending on the architecture.

  • RU: radio-frequency and lower physical-layer functions.
  • DU: time-critical lower-layer processing, including relevant PHY and MAC functions depending on the split.
  • CU: higher-layer functions, including RRC and PDCP in a common 3GPP split architecture.

The actual placement is an architecture decision. It depends on payload capability, feeder-link capacity and latency, ISL topology, service requirements and the required level of autonomy.

Functional decomposition

04 · Waveform engineering from first principles

A waveform is the structured electromagnetic signal used to transmit digital information over a radio channel. Waveform design determines how bits are represented, how the receiver can recover them, how efficiently spectrum is used, and how robust the link is to noise, interference, fading and Doppler.

From bits to a transmitted signal

  1. Source bits: the digital information to transmit.
  2. Channel coding: adds redundancy so errors can be detected and corrected.
  3. Modulation: maps groups of bits to signal symbols.
  4. Resource mapping: assigns symbols to time and frequency resources.
  5. Waveform generation: creates the time-domain signal.
  6. RF transmission: converts the signal to the required carrier frequency and radiates it.

Modulation

Modulation maps digital bits to symbols. In M-ary modulation, each symbol represents log₂(M) bits before accounting for coding overhead.

ModulationBits per symbolGeneral characteristic
BPSK1Robust, low spectral efficiency.
QPSK2Common robust modulation for satellite and mobile links.
16-QAM4Higher spectral efficiency; requires better signal quality.
64-QAM6Higher throughput; more sensitive to noise and distortion.
256-QAM8High spectral efficiency; requires a strong, clean link.

Higher-order modulation is not automatically better. It delivers more bits per symbol but requires a sufficiently high signal-to-noise ratio and a receiver capable of distinguishing constellation points reliably.

OFDM: Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing

OFDM divides a wideband channel into many closely spaced subcarriers. Each subcarrier carries a modulated stream. The subcarriers are mathematically orthogonal, allowing their spectra to overlap while remaining separable at the receiver when synchronization is maintained.

Rather than generating every subcarrier independently, an OFDM transmitter uses an Inverse Fast Fourier Transform (IFFT) to create the time-domain signal. The receiver uses an FFT to recover the frequency-domain subcarriers.

Why use OFDM?

  • It handles frequency-selective channels well through per-subcarrier equalization.
  • It allows flexible allocation of time-frequency resources.
  • It supports adaptive modulation and coding across resource allocations.
  • It is the basis of the 5G NR downlink waveform.

What is the cyclic prefix?

The cyclic prefix (CP) copies the end of an OFDM symbol and places that copy at its beginning. If the CP is long enough relative to the channel delay spread, it helps prevent inter-symbol interference and preserves the orthogonality needed for simple frequency-domain equalization.

The CP consumes transmission time without carrying new payload information. A longer CP can improve robustness to delay spread but reduces useful efficiency.

Subcarrier spacing and numerology

In 5G NR, subcarrier spacing is configurable. Common values include 15, 30, 60 and 120 kHz, with additional options in relevant frequency ranges and configurations.

Δf = 15 × 2^μ kHz NR numerology relationship for the common scalable subcarrier-spacing family.

Increasing subcarrier spacing shortens the useful OFDM symbol duration. This can help with phase noise and Doppler sensitivity, but it changes the timing structure and does not remove the need for Doppler compensation.

DFT-s-OFDM

DFT-spread OFDM applies a DFT to the data symbols before mapping them to subcarriers. It is used in NR uplink configurations because it can provide lower peak-to-average power ratio than conventional CP-OFDM under relevant conditions.

PAPR: Peak-to-Average Power Ratio

OFDM signals can have high instantaneous peaks because multiple subcarriers add together. PAPR measures the ratio between the peak signal power and average signal power.

PAPR = Ppeak / Paverage Usually expressed in dB as 10 log₁₀(Ppeak / Paverage).

High PAPR is important because a power amplifier must accommodate peaks without excessive nonlinear distortion. Operating the amplifier with more back-off reduces distortion but also reduces power efficiency.

OFDM is not automatically robust to large uncorrected frequency offsets.

  • OFDM uses orthogonal subcarriers and IFFT/FFT processing.
  • The cyclic prefix mitigates delay-spread-induced inter-symbol interference.

05 · The receiver chain: how the bits come back

The receiver performs the inverse operations needed to recover the transmitted information. In a real system, the chain also estimates and compensates for imperfections introduced by the channel, RF hardware, oscillators and motion.

RF input
→
Downconversion
→
AGC / filtering
→
Synchronization
→
FFT
→
Equalization
→
Decode

1. RF reception and downconversion

The antenna receives the radio signal. The RF front end filters and amplifies it, then downconverts it to an intermediate frequency or baseband representation suitable for digital processing.

2. Automatic Gain Control (AGC)

AGC adjusts receiver gain to keep the signal within the useful dynamic range of subsequent analogue and digital stages. Poor gain control can cause clipping or inadequate use of the converter's dynamic range.

3. Synchronization

The receiver needs to establish when symbols begin and where the carrier frequency is. In a moving satellite link, frequency and timing uncertainty can be significant.

  • Timing synchronization: identifies symbol and frame boundaries.
  • Carrier-frequency synchronization: estimates and compensates for frequency offset.
  • Frame synchronization: identifies the structure and boundaries of transmitted frames.
  • Phase tracking: follows residual phase variations during reception.

4. FFT and channel estimation

In an OFDM receiver, the FFT converts the received time-domain samples into frequency-domain subcarriers. Reference signals or pilots help estimate the channel response. The receiver uses that estimate to compensate for channel amplitude and phase distortion.

5. Equalization and demodulation

Equalization compensates for the estimated channel. Demodulation estimates which transmitted constellation symbols are most likely, producing soft information often represented as Log-Likelihood Ratios (LLRs).

6. Forward Error Correction

The decoder uses the redundancy added by the transmitter to correct errors. In NR, LDPC coding is used for the main data channels, while Polar coding is used for key control channels.

7. CRC and higher layers

A Cyclic Redundancy Check (CRC) helps determine whether the decoded block passes an error-detection check. Successfully decoded data is then passed to higher protocol layers.

Why Doppler is a receiver problem

A satellite moving relative to the terminal changes the apparent carrier frequency. If not compensated, the offset can disrupt OFDM subcarrier orthogonality and degrade demodulation.

Δf = (vᵣ / c) × f꜀ First-order Doppler relationship. vᵣ is radial relative velocity, c is the speed of light, and f꜀ is carrier frequency.

The radial component matters: the maximum Doppler shift occurs when relative motion is aligned with the line of sight. The value changes during the pass. The rate of change of Doppler (Doppler rate) also matters for tracking and synchronization.

ILLUSTRATIVE CALCULATION

At a 20 GHz carrier frequency, a radial velocity of 7.5 km/s gives:

Δf ≈ (7,500 / 300,000,000) × 20,000,000,000

Δf ≈ 500 kHz

This is an illustrative high radial-velocity calculation, not a fixed Doppler value for every LEO link. Actual geometry determines the instantaneous radial velocity.

What a system engineer should ask the receiver team

  • What Doppler and Doppler-rate envelope must the receiver tolerate?
  • What residual frequency error is acceptable after compensation?
  • What are the acquisition time and tracking requirements?
  • What signal conditions are used to estimate the channel?
  • What are the receiver's sensitivity, implementation loss and decoding thresholds?
  • How does the receiver recover after loss of synchronization or a beam transition?
Explain the receiver chain in one minute.
“The RF front end receives and downconverts the signal. AGC and filtering condition it. The receiver acquires timing and carrier-frequency synchronization, compensates Doppler and phase errors, and then performs FFT processing for OFDM. It estimates the channel, equalizes the subcarriers, demodulates the symbols into soft bits, applies FEC decoding and checks the result with CRC before passing the data to higher layers.”
  • Receiver chain: RF → downconversion → AGC/filtering → synchronization → FFT → channel estimation → equalization → demodulation → FEC → CRC.
  • Frequency offset and timing error can destroy OFDM performance.
  • Doppler depends on radial relative velocity, not simply satellite speed.
  • Doppler rate matters as well as instantaneous Doppler.
  • LDPC is used for NR data channels; Polar coding is used for key control channels.

06 · Link budget: connect the waveform to the system

A link budget accounts for transmitted power, antenna gains, propagation losses, receiver characteristics and noise. Its purpose is to determine whether the receiver can achieve the required performance with sufficient margin.

The basic link budget

Pr = EIRP + Gr − Lpath − Lother All quantities in dB-compatible units. Pr is received power; EIRP is equivalent isotropically radiated power; Gr is receive antenna gain; Lpath and Lother are losses.

The exact accounting depends on the reference points and whether gains and losses are expressed in dB, dBi or dBm. Be consistent about units and reference planes.

Key terms

TermMeaningWhy it matters
EIRPEquivalent Isotropically Radiated Power.Effective transmitted power including antenna gain and feeder losses.
G/TAntenna gain divided by system noise temperature.Receiver figure of merit; higher is generally better.
FSPLFree-space path loss.Captures geometric spreading of the radio signal.
C/N₀Carrier-to-noise-density ratio.Signal quality relative to noise spectral density.
Eb/N₀Energy per information bit to noise density.Useful for comparing modulation/coding performance.
Implementation lossDifference between ideal and practical receiver performance.Accounts for real-world impairments.
Link marginAvailable performance margin above the required threshold.Provides robustness against uncertainty and changing conditions.

Free-space path loss

FSPL(dB) = 20 log₁₀(4πd / λ) Equivalent common form: 92.45 + 20 log₁₀(f in GHz) + 20 log₁₀(d in km).

Path loss increases with both distance and frequency. A higher carrier frequency can provide more available bandwidth, but it also increases free-space path loss for the same distance and brings additional atmospheric and rain-attenuation considerations.

C/N₀ and Eb/N₀

C/N₀ (dB-Hz) = C/N (dB) + 10 log₁₀(B) B is the noise bandwidth in Hz.
Eb/N₀ = C/N₀ − 10 log₁₀(Rb) Rb is the bit rate in bits per second, with consistent dB units.

The required Eb/N₀ depends on modulation, coding rate, code implementation, target error rate and receiver performance. A link budget therefore cannot be completed independently of the waveform and modem design.

Rain attenuation and availability

Rain attenuation is especially relevant at higher microwave frequencies. Its impact depends on frequency, polarization, location, elevation angle, rain statistics and system design. A system may use fade margins, adaptive coding and modulation, power control, gateway diversity or other mitigation techniques.

Link budget is an iterative design process

  1. Define service requirements: throughput, availability, latency and coverage.
  2. Define the operating frequency, bandwidth and waveform.
  3. Determine satellite and terminal antenna characteristics.
  4. Calculate geometry-dependent propagation losses.
  5. Estimate noise, interference and receiver performance.
  6. Determine the required modulation and coding threshold.
  7. Calculate margin and assess availability across operating conditions.
  8. Iterate the design if the performance target is not met.
If the link budget is short by 2 dB, what can you do?
Do not immediately say “increase transmit power.” Identify the limiting mechanism and consider several options: antenna gain, EIRP, receive G/T, coding rate, modulation order, bandwidth, power allocation, beamforming, interference reduction, diversity, terminal characteristics and implementation loss. Each option has costs or constraints. Explain which subsystem owns each possible improvement.
  • EIRP describes the effective transmit power including antenna gain.
  • G/T is a key receiver figure of merit.
  • FSPL depends on distance and frequency.
  • C/N₀ and Eb/N₀ connect received signal quality to data rate and waveform performance.
  • Link margin must account for real operating conditions and uncertainty.
  • Link budget, waveform and receiver design must be assessed together.

What changes in NTN?

  • Longer propagation delays than terrestrial radio access.
  • Significant Doppler and Doppler-rate effects in LEO.
  • Moving coverage areas and satellite/beam transitions.
  • Different timing-advance and synchronization requirements.
  • Potentially constrained feeder links and gateway visibility.
  • Different assumptions about channel variation and measurement freshness.

08 · Layer 2, MAC and radio scheduling

This is a particularly important preparation area given the publicly visible technical backgrounds of the interviewers. Layer 2 coordinates the use of radio resources and provides services between the physical layer and higher protocol layers.

MAC: Medium Access Control

MAC decides how available radio resources are allocated among users and logical channels. It is a central part of the scheduler.

A scheduler may consider:

  • Channel quality: how well each UE can currently receive or transmit.
  • Buffer state: how much data is waiting to be transmitted.
  • QoS: latency, priority, reliability and throughput requirements.
  • Fairness: how resources are distributed between users.
  • Radio constraints: power, interference, bandwidth and available resource blocks.
  • Mobility: changing beams, satellite geometry and predicted link conditions.

What is a resource block?

In NR, the scheduler allocates resources in time and frequency. A physical resource block (PRB) spans 12 consecutive subcarriers in frequency over the relevant allocation. The exact time duration depends on numerology and slot configuration.

Scheduling objectives

AlgorithmBasic ideaTrade-off
Round RobinDistributes opportunities in sequence.Simple fairness, but ignores channel quality.
Max C/IFavors users with the strongest instantaneous channel.High spectral efficiency, potentially poor fairness.
Proportional FairBalances current achievable rate against historical throughput.Balances efficiency and fairness, depending on configuration.
QoS-awareConsiders service priority and latency constraints.More complex; must balance competing requirements.

Proportional Fair scheduling

A simplified proportional-fair metric compares the achievable instantaneous rate of a user with its historical average throughput.

PF metric ≈ Rᵢ(t) / Tᵢ(t) Rᵢ(t) is the current achievable rate; Tᵢ(t) is the user's average throughput over a configured history window.

A user with a good instantaneous channel may be selected, but a user that has received less service historically can also receive priority. Real schedulers can include QoS weights, queue state, retransmissions, power limits and many additional constraints.

Link adaptation

Link adaptation adjusts modulation and coding to match radio conditions. A UE with a strong channel may use a higher modulation order and coding rate. A UE with a weaker channel may need a more robust modulation and coding scheme.

The network often uses Channel Quality Indicator (CQI) reports and other measurements to inform its decisions. The mapping between reported channel quality and a modulation-and-coding scheme depends on implementation and configuration.

HARQ

Hybrid Automatic Repeat Request combines error correction with retransmission. If a transport block is not decoded successfully, the system may request retransmission. Soft information from multiple transmissions can be combined to improve decoding probability.

In NTN, propagation delay and timing constraints can affect HARQ process design and operation. It is inaccurate to say HARQ is universally disabled in NTN; the configuration and adaptations depend on the system.

  • SDAP: maps QoS flows from the 5G system to data radio bearers.

RRC and mobility

RRC configures the UE, manages radio connections, configures measurements and coordinates mobility procedures. It interacts with lower layers but is not itself a Layer 2 protocol.

How LEO changes scheduling

A terrestrial scheduler often operates with a comparatively stable cell topology. In LEO, the serving satellite and beam may change, the channel geometry evolves, and the time between measurement and use can become important.

  • Stale CSI: channel measurements may no longer represent the channel when resources are used.
  • Predictive scheduling: ephemeris and predicted geometry may help anticipate link evolution.
  • Beam transitions: scheduler decisions may need coordination with beam management.
  • Gateway constraints: user-link resources may be available while feeder-link capacity is constrained.
  • QoS: critical services may require explicit latency, priority and continuity handling.
Explain the difference between scheduling and link adaptation.
Scheduling determines which user receives which time-frequency resources. Link adaptation determines the modulation and coding scheme appropriate for the channel conditions on the allocated resources. They interact: the scheduler uses channel and QoS information, while link adaptation estimates the rate and reliability achievable for a given allocation.
How does MAC interact with PHY?
MAC decides resource allocations and transport-block parameters. PHY implements the transmission and reception using modulation, coding, resource mapping and radio measurements. PHY feedback such as CQI, HARQ outcomes and measurements informs MAC decisions. MAC configuration in turn determines what PHY transmits and receives.
  • MAC schedules resources; PHY transmits and receives them.
  • Scheduling uses channel quality, buffer state, QoS and fairness.
  • Link adaptation selects modulation and coding based on radio conditions.
  • HARQ combines error correction and retransmission; do not claim it is always disabled in NTN.
  • RLC AM uses ARQ; UM does not.
  • PDCP handles functions including ciphering and reordering.
  • RRC is Layer 3, not Layer 2.
  • LEO adds changing geometry, beam transitions and measurement-freshness challenges.

09 · MIMO, Massive MIMO and beamforming

Multiple Input Multiple Output (MIMO) uses multiple transmit and/or receive antennas to improve communication performance. Depending on the channel and implementation, it can improve throughput, reliability or spatial coverage.

Three useful MIMO concepts

  • Spatial multiplexing: sends multiple independent data streams to increase throughput.
  • Diversity: uses multiple signal paths or antennas to improve robustness against fading.
  • Beamforming: combines antenna signals with controlled amplitude and phase to direct energy spatially.

What is Massive MIMO?

Massive MIMO uses a large number of antenna elements to serve multiple users and shape the spatial characteristics of radio transmission. It can increase spectral efficiency and enable more flexible spatial resource allocation.

Channel State Information (CSI)

CSI describes the radio channel between transmit and receive antenna elements. It can include amplitude and phase information and is used for beamforming, precoding, scheduling and link adaptation.

CSI can be obtained through reference signals and feedback, depending on duplexing mode and system design. The quality and freshness of CSI are important: inaccurate channel knowledge can lead to poor precoding and resource allocation.

Channel correlation

If antenna channels are highly correlated, the system may have fewer independent spatial dimensions available. Lower correlation can help support spatial multiplexing, although the actual result also depends on signal-to-noise ratio, antenna configuration, propagation and user geometry.

Antenna selection

Antenna selection chooses a subset of available antenna elements or configurations to achieve a target performance with lower complexity, power consumption or hardware cost.

This is relevant to Frédéric Challita's publicly described research background: Massive MIMO channel characterization and propagation-based antenna-selection strategies. A useful interview discussion would connect propagation characteristics to the practical performance of an antenna configuration.

Beamforming in a satellite system

Beamforming can concentrate energy into a geographic region or toward a particular user. A satellite may use multiple beams to reuse spectrum across different geographic areas, subject to interference and antenna constraints.

  • Coverage: which geographic area a beam serves.
  • Beam gain: the antenna gain in a particular direction.
  • Beam overlap: relevant to transitions and interference.
  • Frequency reuse: increases capacity but requires interference management.
  • Beam hopping: dynamically allocates time and capacity to beams based on demand.

What is the difference between beamforming and beam hopping?
Beamforming controls the spatial direction and shape of transmitted or received energy. Beam hopping changes which beams are illuminated or served over time to allocate capacity dynamically. They are related but not the same function.
Why is CSI freshness important in a LEO system?
The channel changes as the satellite and terminal geometry evolves. If the channel estimate is old, precoding, beamforming, link adaptation and scheduling decisions may be based on an inaccurate channel. The system must consider measurement periodicity, feedback delay, prediction and the rate of channel variation.
  • MIMO can provide spatial multiplexing, diversity and beamforming benefits.
  • CSI is used for precoding, beamforming, scheduling and link adaptation.
  • Channel correlation affects available spatial degrees of freedom.
  • Antenna selection can reduce complexity while retaining target performance.
  • Beamforming is spatial control; beam hopping is time-based beam allocation.
  • CSI freshness becomes important when geometry and channels evolve quickly.

10 · Mobility, beam switching and handover

In terrestrial networks, a UE may move between relatively fixed cells. In LEO, the satellites and their beams move relative to the Earth, and the serving relationship can change even when the terminal is stationary.

Important distinctions

  • Beam switching: changing from one beam to another, potentially within the same satellite.
  • Satellite handover: changing the serving satellite.
  • Gateway switching: changing the ground gateway used for feeder connectivity.
  • Core-network mobility: maintaining the relevant network session and service as access changes.

These are related but not interchangeable. A beam transition does not necessarily imply a core-network relocation, and a gateway transition is not necessarily the same event as a user-link handover.

What makes a successful handover?

  1. Identify the need for transition using measurements, geometry or prediction.
  2. Determine a suitable target beam or satellite.
  3. Prepare target resources and configuration.
  4. Coordinate timing and access to the target radio link.
  5. Transfer or maintain the relevant user-plane and control-plane context.
  6. Confirm service continuity and release old resources when safe.

Predictive mobility

Satellite ephemeris provides information about satellite position and motion. Combined with terminal location, antenna coverage and network configuration, it can help predict future visibility and handover opportunities.

Prediction can reduce dependence on purely reactive measurements, but it does not remove the need to handle real-world uncertainties such as blockage, interference, hardware limitations and unexpected link degradation.

Failure cases to consider

  • Target beam unavailable or overloaded.
  • Handover preparation takes too long.
  • Synchronization fails on the target link.
  • Radio measurements are stale or inaccurate.
  • Feeder link or gateway is unavailable.
  • Satellite or beam resource allocation changes unexpectedly.
  • Control-plane transition succeeds but user-plane continuity fails.
How would you design a handover strategy for a LEO network?
Begin with service continuity requirements. Identify the relevant mobility events and entities: UE, beam, satellite, gateway and core network. Define the measurements and prediction inputs, target-selection criteria, resource preparation, timing, control-plane procedures, user-plane continuity and failure recovery. Include operational scenarios and verification cases.
  • Beam switching, satellite handover and gateway switching are different events.
  • LEO mobility exists even for a stationary terminal.
  • Ephemeris can support predictive mobility decisions.
  • Handover must preserve service continuity, not merely establish a new radio link.
  • Consider target-resource availability, timing, synchronization and failure recovery.

11 · Systems engineering: how you make the architecture real

This is where your architecture and software experience should become a differentiator. The role explicitly includes system requirements, trade studies, CONOPS, technical baselines, interfaces, suppliers and integration.

Requirements hierarchy

System requirements describe what the complete system must achieve. They are decomposed into subsystem requirements that can be allocated to the payload, ground segment, user terminal, network and operations domains.

What makes a good requirement?

  • Unambiguous: only one reasonable interpretation.
  • Verifiable: there is a defined way to prove compliance.
  • Traceable: linked to a higher-level need and relevant lower-level requirements.
  • Feasible: technically and economically achievable.
  • Necessary: it contributes to a defined system objective.
  • Consistent: it does not conflict with other requirements.

Requirements traceability

Traceability links each requirement to its source, design allocation, interface, verification method and verification evidence. It helps prevent requirements from being lost during decomposition or changed without impact analysis.

Interface definition

Complex systems fail at subsystem boundaries as often as they fail inside individual components. Interface engineering makes those boundaries explicit.

CONOPS

A CONOPS describes how the system will be used and operated. It should cover normal operations and relevant off-nominal conditions.

For IRIS², scenarios could include:

  • Normal user access through a serving satellite and gateway.
  • Beam or satellite transition.
  • Gateway loss and recovery.
  • ISL loss or rerouting.
  • Satellite failure and service reconfiguration.
  • Congestion or regional demand surge.
  • Degraded service and restoration.
  • Security or emergency-service operating conditions.

Trade studies

A trade study compares design alternatives against agreed criteria. It should not be a subjective discussion ending with the option someone prefers.

  1. Define the problem and decision to be made.
  2. Identify viable alternatives.
  3. Define criteria and constraints.
  4. Assign weights only where justified and agreed.
  5. Assess each option using evidence and assumptions.
  6. Perform sensitivity analysis where appropriate.
  7. Document the decision, rationale, risks and consequences.

Verification versus validation

VERIFICATION

Did we build the system right?

Does the delivered design meet the specified requirements?

VALIDATION

Did we build the right system?

Does the system meet the intended operational need in its real use context?

Verification methods

  • Test: operate the system and measure its behavior.
  • Analysis: demonstrate compliance through calculations or models.
  • Inspection: examine a design, document or physical implementation.
  • Demonstration: show that a function works in a representative scenario.

Supplier engineering

The system engineer does not need to implement every subsystem personally. The responsibility is to ensure that suppliers understand the requirements, deliver compliant designs and provide evidence that the interfaces and performance are correct.

Typical supplier engineering activities:

  • Technical requirements review and clarification.
  • Preliminary and critical design reviews.
  • Interface definition and change management.
  • Technical risk and assumption tracking.
  • Verification planning and evidence review.
  • Integration planning and anomaly resolution.
  • Configuration and technical-baseline management.

A supplier says its modem meets the requirement. What do you do?
Ask for the exact requirement and verification evidence. Check assumptions, operating conditions, interfaces, configuration and test setup. Review performance margins, not just pass/fail results. Establish the change request and rationale. Identify affected requirements, interfaces, suppliers, software and hardware baselines, verification cases, schedules and risks.   • Verification = built to specification; validation = fit for operational purpose.
  • Supplier compliance requires objective evidence, not assurances.
  • Maintain a controlled technical baseline and assess changes systematically.

Interview questions: likely technical discussion

Use these as oral exercises. Aim for a structured answer: define the concept, explain the mechanism, identify the trade-off, then connect it to IRIS². A concise answer with a clear engineering chain is stronger than a long list of disconnected technical terms.

System and architecture

1. Describe an end-to-end LEO satellite communication system.

Describe the user terminal, user link, satellite payload, feeder link, gateway, terrestrial transport, core network and service platform. Explain how the traffic path changes with transparent versus regenerative payload and how ISLs can alter the routing topology.

2. What are the key architectural trade-offs in a LEO constellation?

Discuss orbit and constellation design, coverage, capacity, latency, satellite count, spectrum, terminal capability, gateway distribution, ISL use, mobility, resilience, cost and operational complexity.

3. What is the difference between a user link and a feeder link?

The user link connects the user terminal to the satellite. The feeder link connects the satellite to a gateway. Their antenna characteristics, link budgets, traffic aggregation and operational constraints differ.

PHY and waveform

4. Explain OFDM and why it is used in 5G NR.

OFDM divides the channel into orthogonal subcarriers. IFFT creates the time-domain waveform and FFT separates subcarriers at the receiver. It supports flexible resource allocation and efficient equalization, but requires accurate synchronization and can have high PAPR.

5. What happens if the Doppler shift is not compensated?

Carrier-frequency offset can reduce OFDM subcarrier orthogonality, create inter-carrier interference and degrade channel estimation, demodulation and decoding. The receiver needs acquisition, tracking and compensation appropriate to the expected Doppler and Doppler rate.

6. What is the relationship between modulation, coding and throughput?

Higher modulation order increases bits per symbol. Higher coding rate increases the proportion of useful information bits. Both can increase throughput but require better channel conditions. The actual throughput also depends on bandwidth, overhead, scheduling and retransmissions.

7. What is the purpose of the cyclic prefix?

It provides a guard interval that helps absorb channel delay spread and reduce inter-symbol interference, allowing frequency-domain equalization under the relevant channel assumptions. It consumes transmission time.

Receiver and link budget

8. Walk through a digital receiver chain.

RF reception, downconversion, filtering and AGC, timing and carrier synchronization, Doppler compensation, FFT, channel estimation, equalization, demodulation, soft-bit generation, FEC decoding and CRC.

9. What is the difference between C/N, C/N₀ and Eb/N₀?

C/N compares carrier power with noise power in a specified bandwidth. C/N₀ compares carrier power with noise spectral density. Eb/N₀ relates energy per bit to noise spectral density and is useful for comparing modulation and coding performance.

10. How would you improve a weak link budget?

Investigate EIRP, antenna gain, G/T, propagation loss, interference, bandwidth, modulation and coding, implementation loss and margin. Consider improvements across multiple subsystems rather than assuming that transmit power is the only lever.

RAN, MAC and MIMO

11. What does a MAC scheduler do?

It allocates time-frequency resources among users and logical channels, considering channel quality, buffer state, QoS, fairness, power and retransmission constraints.

12. Explain proportional-fair scheduling.

It balances instantaneous achievable rate against historical average throughput. It aims to balance spectral efficiency and user fairness. Real implementations can add QoS and other constraints.

13. What is the difference between scheduling and link adaptation?

Scheduling chooses which user receives which radio resources. Link adaptation selects modulation and coding for the radio conditions associated with those resources.

14. Explain Massive MIMO and channel state information.

Massive MIMO uses many antenna elements to improve spatial processing, capacity and robustness. CSI describes the radio channel and informs precoding, beamforming, scheduling and link adaptation.

15. How does LEO change the scheduler's problem?

Channel measurements can become stale, geometry changes, beams and satellites move, and the feeder link may constrain service. Scheduling may need predictive information, mobility coordination and robust adaptation to changing link conditions.

Systems engineering and delivery

16. How do you turn a system need into a subsystem requirement?

Define the operational need, derive measurable system requirements, decompose the function, allocate requirements to subsystems, define interfaces, establish verification methods and maintain traceability.

17. How do you manage conflicting supplier designs?

Return to the system requirements and controlled interfaces. Identify assumptions and constraints, assess system-level impact, compare alternatives, document the decision and update the technical baseline.

18. How do you manage technical changes?

Use formal change control, impact analysis, technical authority approval, configuration management, updated requirements and interfaces, and regression verification.

19. What is the difference between verification and validation?

Verification demonstrates that the system meets its specified requirements. Validation demonstrates that the system meets the intended operational need.

20. How do you handle a requirement that cannot be verified?

Clarify the intended behavior and measurable acceptance criteria. Rewrite the requirement to remove ambiguity, define operating conditions and tolerances, and agree on an appropriate verification method before implementation proceeds.

Personal experience and credibility

21. Why are you interested in this role?

Connect your satellite communications background with your software architecture and integration experience. Explain that you want to return to satellite communications in a system-level role that combines radio, network architecture and supplier integration.

22. What is your strongest contribution to this team?

Your strongest defensible positioning is the ability to work across communication-system boundaries: understand the architecture, make interfaces explicit, translate needs into requirements and coordinate integration across technical specialists and suppliers.

23. Have you personally implemented PHY receiver algorithms?

Answer truthfully. If you have not implemented them, say so directly. Then explain your actual level of involvement in system definition, performance assessment, architecture, integration or technical review. Demonstrate that you understand the receiver chain and can work effectively with the specialists who implement it.
