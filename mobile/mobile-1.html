Orientation: LEO, MEO, IRIS² & Starlink

This page sets vocabulary only — no direct-to-device content yet. Before comparing how IRIS² and Starlink each approach mobile connectivity, you need the two systems compared at the level they actually differ most: constellation shape, orbit choice, and who builds and operates what.

1. Orbital regimes, refresher

Three altitude bands matter for this track. Low Earth orbit runs roughly 160–2,000 km. Medium Earth orbit runs from there out to just below geostationary, roughly 2,000–35,786 km. Geostationary orbit sits at a single altitude, 35,786 km, where orbital period exactly matches Earth's rotation and the satellite appears fixed in the sky. Orbital mechanics and Walker-pattern coverage geometry are owned by M2 — this section only re-establishes the numbers needed to place IRIS² and Starlink correctly.

Period and velocity fall as altitude rises — a satellite closer to Earth must move faster to avoid falling, and completes an orbit sooner.

2. IRIS² at the constellation level

IRIS² is a multi-orbit constellation, not a single shell. The baseline design under the SpaceRISE concession contract is 264 satellites in a "LEO-High" shell at 1,200 km, plus 18 satellites in MEO at 8,000 km — 282 satellites total, funded under a roughly €10.5 billion program with launches originally projected for 2029–2030. A third "LEO-Low" shell of 10 or more satellites at 400–750 km was added for technology incubation, and — most recently — an August 7, 2026 "Rendezvous 1" agreement expanded the program further, to 348 satellites, adding 66 quantum-secured military satellites to the constellation. That expansion is very recent relative to this page's writing and should be treated as a live figure, not a fixed one VERIFY BEFORE CITING.

IRIS² is not attempting to match Starlink's scale. Its purpose is government and institutional resilience — secure, sovereign connectivity that does not depend on a non-European commercial operator — with commercial broadband as a secondary layer, not the primary goal.

3. Starlink at the constellation level

Starlink operates the largest satellite constellation ever built: roughly 10,900+ working satellites in orbit as of mid-August 2026, the great majority in shells near 550 km altitude, flying at approximately 53° inclination. SpaceX holds FCC authorization for 12,000 first-generation satellites and has filed for up to 42,000. Unlike IRIS², Starlink is a single-operator commercial system — no consortium, no separate prime/operator split — and its constellation is continuously replenished: satellites are launched every 2–4 days in batches of 20–29, and older units are actively deorbited at end of life.

4. Operator and prime structure — who builds, who flies, who sells

This is the structural contrast that recurs across the whole track, so fix it now. IRIS² separates three roles: the European Commission and ESA fund and set requirements; a prime industrial consortium — Airbus Defence & Space, Thales Alenia Space, and others — builds the satellites; and a separate consortium of operators, SpaceRISE (SES, Eutelsat, Hispasat), operates the constellation and sells the service. Three different organizations, three different incentive structures, and every interface between them is a contract.

Starlink collapses all three roles into one company. SpaceX designs, builds, launches, operates, and sells Starlink service, end to end. There is no separate prime, no separate operator, no external launch dependency — SpaceX flies Starlink satellites on Starlink's own rockets. That vertical integration is not incidental; it is a structural cost and speed advantage that a consortium model cannot replicate, and it is worth holding in mind every time IRIS²'s slower pace and higher per-satellite cost comes up — the difference is not primarily technical.

5. Why this contrast matters before going further

Everything downstream in this track — payload choices, core-network integration, orchestration — traces back to this structural split. Consortium-built systems optimize for sovereignty, security, and government service guarantees at the cost of speed and unit economics. Vertically integrated commercial systems optimize for speed, scale, and unit economics at the cost of a single point of dependency. Neither is "better" in the abstract — they are answers to different questions, and recognizing which question a given job posting or program is actually answering is a large part of reading the sector correctly.

import math

MU = 398600.4418   # km^3/s^2, Earth standard gravitational parameter
R_E = 6371.0        # km, mean Earth radius

def period_minutes(altitude_km):
    a = R_E + altitude_km
    T = 2 * math.pi * math.sqrt(a**3 / MU)
    return T / 60.0

def velocity_kms(altitude_km):
    a = R_E + altitude_km
    return math.sqrt(MU / a)

for alt in [400, 550, 750, 1200, 8000, 35786]:
    print(f"{alt:>6} km   T={period_minutes(alt):7.2f} min   v={velocity_kms(alt):6.3f} km/s")

Reflection

  1. IRIS²'s three-way split (fund/build/operate) versus Starlink's single vertically integrated company is presented here as a tradeoff, not a verdict. Argue the strongest case that the consortium model is the correct structure for a government-anchored system, even accounting for its cost and speed disadvantage.
  2. The Aug 2026 IRIS² expansion added 66 quantum-secured military satellites to the constellation. What does that addition suggest about how the program's priorities have shifted since the original 2022–2023 concept, and what would you want to verify before treating "348 satellites" as a stable number in an interview?
  3. Starlink's cadence — a new batch every 2–4 days — is only possible because of reusable launch and a single company controlling both the satellites and the rocket. If IRIS²'s consortium wanted to match that cadence, what would have to change structurally, not just technically?
