MOBILE-03

Closing the Link to a Handset

The full derivation and a working Python engine for everything on this page live in M5-6. This page is the plain-language version — read it first if D2D link budgets are new to you, or on its own if you just need the concept without the arithmetic.

1. Why a handset is a hard receiver, in plain terms

M4-1 established the link equation everything in this track rests on: received signal quality (C/N₀) is transmit strength (EIRP) minus path loss minus other losses, plus receiver quality (G/T). A VSAT-class terminal — the kind M4 assumes throughout — has a real dish or flat-panel array: tens of dBi of gain. A smartphone has an antenna built to talk to a cell tower a few kilometres away, not a satellite a thousand kilometres up: close to 0 dBi, sometimes negative.

M5-6 quantifies exactly what that costs you: an unmodified smartphone's EIRP and receive quality (G/T) together fall ~45–47 dB short of a VSAT-class terminal — a factor of tens of thousands in raw signal power. Nothing about the physics changes between a VSAT link and a D2D link; only this one number does, and it changes by a lot.

2. Where the missing decibels come from — four places, plainly

M5-6 breaks the gap into four accounts. In plain terms:

  • Orbit altitude. Flying at 550 km instead of GEO's ~36,000 km recovers roughly 32 dB on its own — the single largest of the four accounts, and the reason D2D at scale is a LEO product, not a GEO one. GEO mobile-satellite service existed for decades before D2D, but only with real terminals — external antennas, real transmit power, users who pointed them at the sky. It never worked with an unmodified phone, because it never had this 32 dB for free.
  • Aperture. The satellite's own antenna has to be much larger, or split into many more, narrower beams, to make up on the transmit side what the handset can't make up on the receive side.
  • Bandwidth. A narrower carrier concentrates the same power into less spectrum, which directly raises the effective signal-to-noise ratio in that narrower channel — the tradeoff being less total data rate.
  • Waveform floor. The modulation and coding chosen for D2D is deliberately robust — able to work at a much lower signal-to-noise ratio than a broadband waveform — at the cost of carrying fewer bits per symbol. This is the D2D equivalent of a GEO system dropping to its most robust MODCOD in bad weather, except D2D runs there by default, not just as a fallback.

Every one of these is a real engineering lever pulled specifically to close a link that the handset's antenna alone cannot close.

3. The downlink is the tight link, not the uplink

In ordinary broadband satcom, the return link (terminal to satellite) is usually the constrained one — the terminal has less power than the satellite. D2D reverses this. The satellite's huge receive aperture more than makes up for the handset's feeble transmitter, so the uplink actually closes comfortably. What doesn't have anything compensating for it is the downlink: the handset's terrible receive quality has no aperture on its side to help. So counterintuitively, sending a message from your phone to the satellite is the easy direction — getting a reply back is the hard one.

4. Doppler and pass geometry, why LEO forces the issue

A LEO satellite is moving at roughly 7.6 km/s. At a typical D2D handset frequency near 1.9 GHz, that motion alone produces a frequency shift of around 48 kHz at the start and end of a pass — large relative to how narrow a resource block is, not a rounding error. And the pass itself is short: roughly eight minutes overhead, at a 550 km altitude, before the satellite drops back below a useful elevation angle. Every D2D architecture has to solve both problems — correct for a shift that size, and hand a session off to the next satellite every few minutes — and how each one solves them is exactly what separates the architectures compared in MOBILE-2.

5. What "narrowband by design" means for you as a user

Put the accounts together and the conclusion is structural, not incidental: D2D services are narrowband by design, not by immaturity. That's why the current generation of services carries text messages and short data before it carries video calls — the link budget genuinely doesn't support broadband rates yet, for the same reason a GEO satphone call sounds different from a fibre call. It isn't that the D2D engineers haven't gotten there yet; it's that the accounts above cost real decibels, and broadband-grade data rates cost decibels the link doesn't currently have to spend.

From Zero to 5G NTN: OFDM, Doppler & Synchronization

This lesson starts from absolute zero. Every acronym, every unit, and every physical idea is introduced before it is used.

The Big Picture

A Direct-to-Device (D2D) connection is astonishing because an ordinary smartphone communicates with a satellite travelling about 7.6 km/s. The phone is not redesigned into a satellite terminal; instead, 5G NTN extends the existing 5G radio standard with new synchronization techniques.

Definition 1 — UE (User Equipment)

UE stands for User Equipment. It is the official 3GPP name for the user's device: a smartphone, tablet, wearable or IoT sensor. Throughout the rest of the Mobile curriculum, whenever you see UE, think your phone.

Definition 2 — What Is a Radio Wave?

A radio wave is an electromagnetic wave that oscillates. Imagine the electric field rising and falling smoothly like a sine wave. The number of oscillations completed every second is called the frequency.

UnitMeaning
Hz1 oscillation per second
kHz1,000 Hz
MHz1 million Hz
GHz1 billion Hz

A D2D handset typically operates around 1.9 GHz, meaning the carrier oscillates about 1.9 billion times every second.

Definition 3 — What Is a Carrier?

The carrier is the high-frequency wave that transports information. Voice, video and internet data do not travel directly; they are encoded onto this carrier.

Definition 4 — OFDM

OFDM stands for Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing. Instead of using one large carrier, OFDM divides it into hundreds or thousands of extremely narrow subcarriers that all transmit simultaneously.

Orthogonality

The word orthogonal means the subcarriers are mathematically arranged so they overlap without interfering. They occupy nearly the same spectrum while remaining separable by the receiver.

Definition 5 — Subcarrier

A subcarrier is one of those narrow frequency tones. In a common 5G numerology, adjacent subcarriers are spaced 15 kHz apart.

Definition 6 — Resource Block (RB)

A scheduler never allocates individual subcarriers. It allocates Resource Blocks.

ObjectDefinition
SubcarrierOne narrow tone
Resource Block12 adjacent subcarriers (180 kHz)
SchedulerSoftware that assigns RBs to users

The Doppler Effect

When transmitter and receiver move relative to one another, the received frequency changes. This is called the Doppler effect.

The satellite approaches, reaches overhead, then recedes. The frequency continuously shifts from about +48 kHz to −48 kHz.

Where Does 48 kHz Come From?

The Doppler equation is:

Δf = (v / c) × f

VariableMeaning
vRelative velocity (7,600 m/s)
cSpeed of light (300,000,000 m/s)
fCarrier frequency (1.9 GHz)

Using these parameters yields approximately 48,000 Hz (48 kHz) of frequency shift.

ScenarioDoppler
130 km/h car≈ 228 Hz

The Critical Insight

The phone does not jump from one subcarrier to another. Instead, it continuously adjusts its own internal frequency reference, called the local oscillator.

NTN Synchronization

NTN stands for Non-Terrestrial Network. Release 17 introduced two major additions that terrestrial 5G did not need.

1. Frequency Pre-compensation

The UE knows its GNSS position. The satellite broadcasts its ephemeris, which describes its orbit, position, and velocity. The phone predicts the Doppler before transmitting and intentionally shifts its uplink frequency.

2. Timing Advance

Radio waves travel at the speed of light, but 600 km still takes about 2 milliseconds. Users on opposite sides of the beam have different propagation delays, so each phone transmits slightly early.
