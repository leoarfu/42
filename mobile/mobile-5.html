MOBILE-05

Payload for D2D: What's Actually New

M3-3 owns transparent vs. regenerative in full. M3-4 owns phased-array theory in full, including active arrays by name. This page bridges both, briefly, and spends its real effort on the one piece neither lesson covers by name: the digital transparent processor.

1. Transparent vs. regenerative, recapped in one page

M3-3's distinction in short: transparent payloads amplify and relay without ever converting the signal to bits — simple, waveform-agnostic, but unable to switch traffic between beams on board. Regenerative payloads demodulate, process, and remodulate — capable of on-board switching and routing (what makes inter-satellite-link mesh routing possible at all) at the cost of real hardware complexity and a waveform lock-in the transparent case doesn't have. Read M3-3 in full for the complete tradeoff table; this page assumes you have the shape of it.

2. Active antennas / phased arrays for D2D beamforming, recapped

M3-4 covers phased-array theory, active arrays with per-element transmit/receive modules, and digital beamforming in full. For D2D specifically, the relevant consequence is the one MOBILE-3 §2 already named: a satellite closes the handset gain gap by generating many narrow, high-gain spot beams rather than relying on any gain from the ground device — which is precisely the array-gain-versus-beamwidth tradeoff M3-4 works out with real numbers. AST SpaceMobile's very large arrays are this tradeoff pushed to one extreme: fewer, larger apertures instead of more numerous smaller ones.

3. Digital transparent processors — the actual new content

Industry materials — including Thales Alenia Space's IRIS² program description and independent technical literature from ESA and IEEE — use the term "digital transparent processor." Multiple independent sources confirm what it actually does: it digitizes the uplink signal, slices it into fine bandwidth granules, switches those granules between beams as needed, and recombines them for downlink transmission — all without ever demodulating to bits. ESA's own description calls it "an important component, permitting to adapt and route the traffic according to the market needs" for multibeam mobile and flexible payloads.

That is the same mechanism, not just a similar one, to what M3-3 §3 calls a digital channeliser: it sits genuinely between transparent and regenerative — more flexible than a fixed-hardware bent-pipe (bandwidth can be reallocated between beams from the ground, after launch, in software), without the full cost and waveform lock-in of a true regenerative processor.

What M3-3 doesn't cover, because its examples are GEO VHTS and O3b mPower rather than handset D2D, is what this buys a D2D system specifically: the ability to reallocate the many narrow spot beams from §2 in response to where subscriber demand actually is at a given moment — shifting capacity toward a beam over a city at rush hour, away from a beam over open ocean — without the cost of full onboard demodulation for every one of those beams. That reallocation flexibility, applied to a D2D beam plan specifically, is the genuinely new piece this page adds.

4. Cost, mass, power tradeoffs, D2D-specific framing

M3-3's general cost/mass/complexity ordering — transparent cheapest, channeliser/DTP in the middle, full regenerative most expensive — holds for D2D payloads too. It explains, in payload terms, the same pattern MOBILE-2 already described commercially: Starlink Mobile shipped transparent-payload bent-pipe LTE first because it was cheapest and fastest; IRIS² is building toward regenerative because its governing constraint is capability and sovereignty, not speed to market.
